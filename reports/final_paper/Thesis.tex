\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Music Genre Classification (MGC) is a fundamental task in Music Information Retrieval (MIR) that aims to automatically categorize music tracks into predefined genres based on their audio content. This capability is crucial for a wide range of applications, including music recommendation systems, intelligent library organization, and content-based discovery platforms. The paper by Patil et al. \cite{patil} addresses this task by introducing a deep neural network, described as a U-Net-like architecture. The authors claim state-of-the-art performance, notably an accuracy of 99.41\% on the widely-used GTZAN dataset \textbf{\cite{tzanetakis2002gtzan}}. %<-- Citazione del paper originale di GTZAN.

This project undertakes a formal replication and extension of Patil et al.'s work. Our goals were threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Replicate and Validate:} To implement the core U-Net based architecture and rigorously evaluate its performance on GTZAN, thereby validating the central claim of the paper.
    \item \textbf{Compare and Analyze:} To compare this architecture against two other common CNN designs to understand its relative strengths and weaknesses in terms of both accuracy and computational efficiency.
    \item \textbf{Extend and Generalize:} To assess the model's robustness and adaptability by testing it on three additional, diverse datasets: FMA Small, Indian Music Genre, and Tabla Taala.
\end{enumerate}
Through this structured approach, we aim to provide a transparent, reproducible, and critical analysis of the proposed model, contributing a reliable performance benchmark to the field.

\section{Data Preprocessing and Preparation}
\label{sec:data_preprocessing}

A robust and leak-free data pipeline is the foundation of any reliable machine learning experiment. This section details the datasets used and the standardized preprocessing steps applied to ensure consistency and reproducibility across all experiments.

\subsection{Datasets}
We utilized four distinct datasets to provide a comprehensive evaluation of our models:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GTZAN:} The primary dataset for our replication study, as used in the original paper \cite{patil}. It consists of 1,000 30-second audio clips, balanced across 10 musical genres. Despite its known issues with data quality and potential for leakage, it remains a standard MGC benchmark \textbf{\cite{tzanetakis2002gtzan}}. %<-- Citazione a GTZAN.
    \item \textbf{FMA Small:} A more challenging and realistic benchmark from the Free Music Archive \textbf{\cite{defferrard2017fma}}. %<-- Citazione del paper originale di FMA.
    It contains 8,000 tracks balanced across 8 diverse genres.
    \item \textbf{Indian Music Genre (IMG):} A dataset focused on non-Western music, featuring 5 genres from the Indian musical tradition \textbf{\cite{kaggleIndianMusic}}. %<-- Citazione alla fonte del dataset su Kaggle.
    \item \textbf{Tabla Taala:} A specialized rhythmic dataset containing recordings of 8 different North Indian rhythmic cycles played on the tabla \textbf{\cite{kaggleTablaTaala}}. %<-- Citazione alla fonte del dataset su Kaggle.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Feature Extraction: Mel-Spectrograms}
CNNs are primarily designed to process image data. Therefore, to leverage their powerful feature extraction capabilities, we converted the one-dimensional audio waveforms into two-dimensional representations. Our chosen feature representation is the \textbf{Mel-spectrogram}, a variant of the standard spectrogram that is perceptually motivated. It represents the spectral power density of a signal over time, but with the frequency axis mapped to the Mel scale, which approximates the non-linear frequency response of the human ear. This makes the representation more aligned with how humans perceive sound and often leads to better performance in audio tasks.

The generation process for each audio clip was as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Loading and Framing:} The audio signal was loaded and divided into short, overlapping frames.
    \item \textbf{STFT:} A Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) was applied to each frame to compute its frequency spectrum. We used a window size (n\_fft) of 2048 samples and a hop length of 512 samples.
    \item \textbf{Mel Scale Conversion:} The power spectrum from the STFT was mapped onto the Mel scale using a filter bank of 128 Mel filters.
    \item \textbf{Logarithmic Scaling:} The resulting amplitudes were converted to a logarithmic scale (decibels), which compresses the dynamic range and aligns better with human perception of loudness.
\end{enumerate}
The final output for each 30-second track was a Mel-spectrogram image, visually representing its musical content, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:spectrogram}.

% --------- Detailed Mel parameters (added for reproducibility)
\paragraph{Mel-spectrogram parameters}
For reproducibility, the exact parameters used to compute the Mel-spectrograms are listed below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item sample rate = 22,050 Hz
    \item n\_fft = 2048
    \item hop\_length = 512
    \item n\_mels = 128
    \item window = Hann
    \item power = 2.0 (power spectrogram) and conversion to decibels via \texttt{librosa.power\_to\_db(ref=np.max)}
    \item handling of duration: audio shorter than 3s windows were zero-padded, longer signals were truncated to exact clip length prior to STFT
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Dataset-specific STFT settings}
For the Tabla Taala experiments we used a shorter FFT window (n\_fft = 1024) as configured in the Tabla notebooks; all other experiments used n\_fft = 2048 as stated above.

\subsection{Dataset Augmentation and Partitioning}
While the GTZAN dataset contains 1,000 tracks, this is a relatively small number for training deep learning models, posing a risk of overfitting. To address this, we employed a common data augmentation strategy:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Slicing:} Each 30-second spectrogram was sliced into 10 non-overlapping 3-second segments. This technique increased the total number of training samples tenfold, from 1,000 to \textbf{10,000}, providing the models with a much larger and more varied dataset to learn from.
\end{itemize}

Following augmentation, the dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and test sets using a standard 60/20/20 split. A \textbf{stratified splitting} strategy was employed to ensure that the proportion of samples for each genre was maintained across all three sets. This is a vital step to guarantee that the model is evaluated on a representative data distribution. The final dataset structure was:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{6,000 training samples} used for model learning.
    \item \textbf{2,000 validation samples} used for hyperparameter tuning and monitoring for early stopping.
    \item \textbf{2,000 test samples} held out for the final, unbiased evaluation of the model's generalization performance.
\end{itemize}
All resulting arrays were saved as NumPy files (\texttt{.npy}) for efficient data loading during the training phase.

% --------- Clarify slicing vs split to avoid leakage
\paragraph{Slicing and splitting protocol (avoiding leakage)}
To prevent any form of data leakage, all dataset partitioning was performed at the original track level prior to slicing. The 1000 GTZAN audio files were first stratified by genre and split into train/validation/test sets (60/20/20) at the track granularity; only after this split did we slice each track in a given partition into non-overlapping 3-second segments. This guarantees that segments from the same original audio file cannot appear in multiple partitions and prevents the model from exploiting track-level fingerprint information.

\subsection{Label Encoding}
The textual genre labels (e.g., "Rock", "Blues") were converted into a numerical format suitable for the model's loss function. We used a simple integer encoding scheme, mapping each of the 10 genres to a unique integer from 0 to 9. This mapping was created and saved using the \texttt{pickle} library. Storing the encoder is a crucial step for reproducibility, as it ensures that the label-to-integer mapping remains consistent across all experiments and during inference. For instance, it guarantees that "Rock" is always encoded as the same integer (e.g., 8) every time the code is executed.

\section{Model Architectures and Training Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
This section details the architectures of the three Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) models implemented for this study, the data augmentation techniques employed, and the standardized training protocol used for evaluation.

\subsection{Model Architectures}
We implemented three distinct CNN architectures to provide a comprehensive comparative analysis. All models were developed using the Keras API with a TensorFlow backend and are defined in our \texttt{ModelFactory}.

\subsubsection{Efficient-VGG}
This model serves as a lightweight baseline, drawing inspiration from the VGG architecture's simplicity and effectiveness. Its design prioritizes computational efficiency while establishing a solid performance benchmark. The architecture comprises:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input Layer:} Accepts Mel-spectrograms of shape (128, 128, 1).
    \item \textbf{Convolutional Blocks (x3):} The model's core consists of three sequential blocks designed to learn hierarchical features. The filter count increases with depth (16, 32, 64) to capture progressively more abstract patterns. Each block follows the structure:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item A \texttt{Conv2D} layer with a 3x3 kernel and ReLU activation.
        \item A \texttt{BatchNormalization} layer to stabilize training dynamics.
        \item A \texttt{MaxPooling2D} layer (2x2) to reduce spatial dimensions.
        \item A \textbf{Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) Block}: This channel-wise attention mechanism is added after each pooling layer. It recalibrates feature maps by learning the importance of each channel, enhancing representational power with minimal computational overhead.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Classification Head:} The final feature maps are processed by a \texttt{GlobalAveragePooling2D} (GAP) layer, followed by a \texttt{Dense} layer (128 neurons, ReLU), a \texttt{Dropout} layer (rate=0.5) for regularization, and a final 10-neuron \texttt{softmax} output layer.
\end{itemize}
With a total of approximately 34,500 parameters, this model is the most compact of the three.

\subsubsection{ResSE-AudioCNN}
This architecture is based on the principles of Residual Networks (ResNets), which excel at training deeper networks by incorporating skip connections to mitigate the vanishing gradient problem.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initial Convolution:} The input is first processed by a standard \texttt{Conv2D} layer with Batch Normalization and a PReLU activation.
    \item \textbf{Residual SE Blocks (x3):} The network backbone is formed by three residual blocks with increasing filter depths (64, 128, 256). Each block contains:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item A main path with two \texttt{Conv2D} layers, each followed by Batch Normalization and a Parametric ReLU (PReLU) activation. PReLU introduces a learnable parameter for the slope of negative inputs, offering more flexibility than standard ReLU.
        \item A Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) block for channel attention.
        \item A \textbf{skip connection} that adds the block's input to its output. A 1x1 convolution is used in the skip path if dimensional projection is required (due to striding).
    \end{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Classification Head:} A GAP layer, Dropout (0.5), and a final softmax layer.
\end{itemize}
This model, with approximately 1.23 million parameters, represents a more complex and powerful architecture.

\subsubsection{U-Net Audio Classifier}
This model is our implementation of the architecture proposed by Patil et al. \cite{patil}, adapted specifically for the classification task. Unlike a traditional U-Net for segmentation \textbf{\cite{ronneberger2015unet}}, %<-- Citazione del paper originale della U-Net.
our model exclusively utilizes the \textbf{encoder (contracting) path} for feature extraction. This design allows the network to learn hierarchical features at multiple spatial resolutions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Encoder Path:} This path consists of a series of down-sampling blocks. We implemented three such blocks plus a final bottleneck block.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Encoder Block (x3):} Each block is designed to extract features and reduce dimensionality. It contains:
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Two consecutive \texttt{Conv2D} layers (3x3 kernel, 'same' padding, PReLU activation) followed by \texttt{BatchNormalization}.
            \item A \texttt{MaxPooling2D} (2x2) layer at the end, which halves the spatial dimensions and doubles the number of feature channels (32 \(\rightarrow\) 64 \(\rightarrow\) 128). This multi-scale approach enables the network to capture both fine-grained textural information and broader contextual patterns.
        \end{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Bottleneck Block:} This final convolutional block (256 filters) processes the feature map from the last encoder block but omits the pooling layer, thus serving as the point of highest feature abstraction before classification.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Classification Head:} The output of the bottleneck is not up-sampled. Instead, it is directly fed into a classification head consisting of:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item A \texttt{GlobalAveragePooling2D} layer to aggregate the feature maps into a single feature vector.
        \item A \texttt{Dropout} layer (rate=0.5) for regularization.
        \item The final \texttt{Dense} output layer with 10 neurons and \texttt{softmax} activation.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
With approximately 1.18 million parameters, this architecture is designed to efficiently capture both local and global context from the spectrograms.

\subsection{Data Augmentation: SpecAugment}
To improve model robustness and prevent overfitting, we employed \textbf{SpecAugment} \textbf{\cite{park2019specaugment}}, %<-- Citazione del paper originale di SpecAugment.
a data augmentation technique applied on-the-fly to the Mel-spectrograms during training. It works by applying masking policies to the input data, forcing the model to learn more generalizable features. The transformations include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Frequency Masking:} A random band of consecutive frequency channels is set to zero. This simulates the loss of specific pitch information, preventing the model from relying excessively on any single frequency range. In our implementation, the maximum size of the mask was set to 20\% of the total frequency bins (i.e., up to 25 of the 128 Mel bands).
    \item \textbf{Time Masking:} A random block of consecutive time steps is masked. This makes the model more robust to temporal occlusions or variations in the audio. The maximum mask size was similarly set to 20\% of the total time steps.
\end{itemize}
The direct impact of this technique was evaluated in our ablation study (WP1), where each model was trained both with and without SpecAugment enabled.

% --------- SpecAugment exact params
\paragraph{SpecAugment parameters}
The SpecAugment configuration used in all experiments was as follows: up to 2 frequency masks and up to 2 time masks per example, with each mask length sampled uniformly up to 20\% of the corresponding axis length. Masks were applied on-the-fly only to training samples. Frequency and time masks could overlap.

\subsection{Training Protocol}
To ensure a fair and reproducible comparison, all models were trained using a standardized protocol:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Optimizer:} The Adam optimizer was used with an initial learning rate of \(1 \times 10^{-3}\).
    \item \textbf{Loss Function:} Categorical Cross-Entropy, as it is standard for multi-class classification.
    \item \textbf{Epochs and Batch Size:} Models were trained for a maximum of 120 epochs with a batch size of 48.
    \item \textbf{Random Seeds and Reproducibility:} Random seeds were fixed to ensure reproducibility across runs: Python seed = 42, NumPy seed = 42, TensorFlow seed = 42. Results reported for the single-split experiments correspond to a single training run with this seed unless otherwise stated; cross-validation results aggregate five independent folds.
    \item \textbf{Training Management:} The training process was managed by a suite of Keras callbacks to ensure robustness and prevent overfitting:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{EarlyStopping}: Monitored the validation accuracy with a patience of 25 epochs. Training was automatically halted if no improvement was observed, and the best weights were restored.
        \item \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau}: Monitored the validation loss and reduced the learning rate by a factor of 0.2 if it plateaued, allowing for finer optimization.
        \item \texttt{ModelCheckpoint}: Saved the best version of the model based on validation accuracy throughout the training process.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Computational Setup:} Training was performed on a GPU, with mixed precision ('mixed\_float16') enabled to accelerate computation and reduce memory consumption.
    \item \textbf{Latency measurement protocol:} Inference latency reported in Table \ref{tab:gtzan_main_results} is the mean time (in milliseconds) to process a single 128\(\times\)128\(\times\)1 input on an NVIDIA GPU (see Appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility} for hardware details). For each model we executed 50 warm-up inferences followed by 200 timed runs (batch size=1) and reported the average.
\end{itemize}

\section{Pushing the Limits: Cross-Dataset Generalization}
\label{sec:generalization}

A model's true strength is revealed not on a single, clean benchmark but on its ability to generalize to new, unseen challenges. To rigorously test the capabilities and limitations of our champion U-Net architecture, we extended our analysis beyond the GTZAN dataset. We selected three additional datasets, each presenting a unique test: FMA Small for complexity and realism, Indian Music Genre for cultural domain shift, and Tabla Taala for a highly specialized rhythmic pattern recognition task. This section details the methodology and findings for each of these experiments.

\subsection{FMA Small: A Test of Complexity and Realism}

\paragraph{Dataset and Rationale}
The Free Music Archive (FMA) Small dataset \textbf{\cite{defferrard2017fma}} %<-- Citazione della fonte del dataset FMA.
represents a significant step up in difficulty from GTZAN. It comprises 8,000 high-quality, 30-second tracks, balanced across 8 genres: \textit{Electronic, Experimental, Folk, Hip-Hop, Instrumental, International, Pop,} and \textit{Rock}. FMA is notoriously challenging for several reasons:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Greater Intra-Genre Variance:} Genres like `Experimental` and `Electronic` encompass a vast range of styles, making it difficult for the model to identify a consistent feature signature.
    \item \textbf{Increased Spectral Overlap:} Unlike the more curated GTZAN, FMA features significant spectral overlap between classes (e.g., `Rock`, `Pop`, and `Folk`), demanding a more nuanced feature extraction capability.
    \item \textbf{Real-World Data:} The data is sourced from a diverse pool of artists and recordings, reflecting a more realistic and less sanitized distribution than typical academic benchmarks.
\end{itemize}
For this experiment, we utilized the official 60/20/20 train/validation/test split.

\paragraph{Methodology}
Our methodology for this experiment, as documented in the notebook \texttt{01\_train.ipynb}, involved training our \texttt{UNet\_Audio\_Classifier} from scratch. Given the substantial size of the FMA dataset, this approach serves as a direct stress test of the architecture's ability to learn from a complex data distribution without the aid of pre-trained weights. The training configuration was adapted slightly for this task: SpecAugment was retained for regularization, but the label smoothing factor was increased to `0.05` to counteract the higher potential for overfitting on this noisy dataset. The model was trained for up to 80 epochs with a patience of 15 on validation accuracy.

\paragraph{Results and Analysis}
The model achieved a final test accuracy of \textbf{41.1\%}. While modest in absolute terms, this performance significantly surpasses the 12.5\% random baseline for an 8-class problem, confirming that the model learned meaningful patterns despite the dataset's complexity. Table \ref{tab:cross_dataset_summary} provides a detailed performance breakdown.

The confusion matrix in Figure \ref{fig:cm_fma} visually illustrates the nature of the model's struggles. The diagonal is weak, indicating systemic confusion. Specifically, the model frequently misclassifies tracks between spectrally adjacent or structurally ambiguous genres. For instance, `Experimental` music is often confused with `Instrumental` and `Electronic`, which is logical given their fluid definitions. This performance highlights a crucial finding: an architecture optimized on a curated dataset like GTZAN requires significant adaptation or a much larger capacity to master the "messy," high-entropy data distribution characteristic of FMA.

\subsection{Indian Music Genre \& Tabla Taala: A Test of Domain Adaptation}

For the Indian and Tabla datasets, we adopted a more sophisticated strategy: \textbf{transfer learning} \textbf{\cite{choi2017transfer}}. %<-- Citazione che supporta il concetto di transfer learning in ambito musicale.
These datasets are considerably smaller than GTZAN, making them highly susceptible to overfitting if a deep model like our U-Net were trained from scratch. Our hypothesis was that the low-level and mid-level features learned by the U-Net on thousands of Western music tracks—such as onsets, timbral textures, and basic harmonic patterns—could provide a powerful feature extractor for these new, musically related tasks.

\paragraph{The Datasets}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Indian Music Genre (IMG):} This dataset contains 500 tracks across 5 genres from the Indian musical tradition (\textit{Sufi, Bollypop, Carnatic, Ghazal, Semiclassical}) \textbf{\cite{kaggleIndianMusic}}, %<-- Citazione fonte dataset.
    providing a test of the model's ability to adapt to different timbral palettes, microtonal variations, and structural conventions.
    \item \textbf{Tabla Taala:} This highly specialized dataset contains 561 recordings of 8 different North Indian rhythmic cycles (taals) played on the tabla \textbf{\cite{kaggleTablaTaala}}. %<-- Citazione fonte dataset.
    This experiment represents a task shift, moving from general genre classification to fine-grained rhythmic pattern recognition based on the percussive patterns of the tabla.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Methodology: Transfer Learning from GTZAN}
As implemented in the notebooks \texttt{01\_train (1).ipynb} for the Indian dataset and \texttt{01\_train (2).ipynb} for the Tabla dataset, our transfer learning protocol was precisely tailored:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We instantiated our champion \texttt{UNet\_Audio\_Classifier} architecture.
    \item The convolutional backbone was initialized by loading the weights from the U-Net model pre-trained on GTZAN (\texttt{WITH\_AUG} version). This step provides a robust set of initial features optimized for audio spectrograms.
    \item The final \texttt{Dense} classification layer was replaced with a new, randomly initialized layer matching the number of classes in the target dataset (5 for IMG, 8 for Tabla).
    \item We then fine-tuned the entire network end-to-end on the new dataset using a lower learning rate (`4e-4` for Indian, `5e-4` for Tabla) and a milder label smoothing factor (`0.01` and `0.02` respectively). This allows the pre-trained features to adapt to the nuances of the new data domains while preventing catastrophic forgetting. For the IMG training, we also utilized the AdamW optimizer with a small weight decay (`1e-5`) for improved regularization.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Comparative Analysis with the Reference Study}
\label{sec:comparison}

A critical objective of this replication study is the direct comparison of our findings with those reported by Patil et al. This analysis serves to validate our implementation and contextualize our results within the landscape established by the original paper. Our findings both corroborate certain architectural claims and, more significantly, reveal substantial discrepancies in key performance metrics. These differences suggest that underlying methodological choices, rather than minor architectural variations, are the primary drivers of the divergent outcomes.

\subsection{Discrepancy in GTZAN Classification Accuracy}
The most significant divergence between our study and the reference paper is the final classification accuracy on the GTZAN dataset. The performance gap is substantial and warrants a detailed examination:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Patil et al. Reported Accuracy:} An exceptionally high \textbf{99.41\%}.
    \item \textbf{Our Best Single-Run Test Accuracy:} A robust \textbf{82.3\%}.
    \item \textbf{Our Most Reliable Score (5-Fold CV Mean):} An excellent \textbf{90.44\%}.
\end{itemize}
Even our most optimistic, cross-validated result presents a performance delta of approximately nine percentage points from the score reported in the paper. While our implementation demonstrates strong and stable performance, it does not approach the near-perfect classification claimed by the authors. This discrepancy strongly suggests that factors beyond the model architecture itself are at play.

\subsection{Investigating Potential Sources of Performance Discrepancy}
While definitive conclusions are impossible without access to the original source code and data partitions, we posit three potential explanations for this variance, ranked in order of perceived likelihood.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data Splitting and Information Leakage (Leading Hypothesis):} Our leading hypothesis centers on the critical issue of data partitioning and the potential for information leakage. The reference study does not specify its data partitioning strategy. A common and severe pitfall in audio classification involves splitting a single 30-second audio track into multiple 3-second segments and then distributing these segments randomly across training, validation, and test sets. This creates an information leak, as the model learns to recognize the unique acoustic fingerprint of a specific track (e.g., the artist's timbre, recording environment, or mastering profile) rather than the generalizable features of a genre \textbf{\cite{salamon2017data_leakage}}. %<-- Citazione chiave che spiega e supporta l'ipotesi di data leakage.
    This effectively transforms the task from genre classification to audio fingerprinting, leading to artificially inflated and misleadingly high performance metrics.

    Our implementation, in contrast, strictly adheres to a track-level split, a more rigorous methodology that ensures no temporal overlap between the sets. Our 5-fold cross-validation protocol further strengthens this approach, providing a more conservative and realistic measure of the model's true generalization capability.

    \item \textbf{Methodological Ambiguity of the "Novel Mathematical Model":} The paper attributes its success to a "novel mathematical model" and "fine-grained features forwarding," but describes these components in abstract terms, lacking the specific architectural and algorithmic details required for faithful replication. Reproducibility is a cornerstone of scientific inquiry, and this ambiguity hinders independent verification. Our adaptation of the U-Net architecture for classification—replacing the decoder path with a global pooling layer and a dense classifier—represents a standard and principled method documented in machine learning literature for converting segmentation models to classification tasks \textbf{\cite{ronneberger2015unet}}. %<-- Ri-citare U-Net qui ha senso per contestualizzare la tua implementazione.
    Without reproducible specifics, it is impossible to ascertain the precise contribution, if any, of their proposed modifications over a standard implementation.

    \item \textbf{Contextualizing the State-of-the-Art on GTZAN:} Finally, the reported accuracy of 99.41\% warrants contextualization within the broader academic literature. The GTZAN dataset, while a foundational benchmark, is known to contain labeling inconsistencies and significant spectral ambiguity between genres such as Rock, Country, and Blues. Consequently, state-of-the-art models published in peer-reviewed venues typically achieve accuracies in the 90-95\% range \textbf{\cite{salamon2017data_leakage}}. %<-- Questa fonte è utile anche qui perché discute le problematiche di GTZAN.
    A near-perfect score is a significant outlier that deviates from established community results. This suggests that the evaluation conditions in the original study may have been exceptionally favorable, potentially due to the data leakage issues discussed previously.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Corroboration of Model Efficiency}
Despite the discrepancy in top-line accuracy, our efficiency analysis (WP2) independently validates the paper's general assertion regarding the architecture's effectiveness. Our experiments confirm that the U-Net based classifier provides a superior balance of performance and computational cost compared to a standard ResNet-based architecture. Specifically, our \texttt{UNet\_Audio\_Classifier} proved both more accurate and computationally more efficient (1.18M parameters; 23.07 ms latency) than the comparable ResSE-AudioCNN model (1.23M parameters; 26.75 ms latency). This finding validates the central premise that the U-Net's multi-scale encoder design is a highly effective choice for spectrogram-based classification tasks.


\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This project embarked on a rigorous replication and extension of the study by Patil et al. \cite{patil}, with the dual objectives of validating their proposed U-Net based architecture and establishing a transparent, reproducible performance baseline for music genre classification. Our investigation yielded a complex set of findings, leading to a nuanced conclusion that both confirms core architectural principles and highlights critical methodological considerations in the field.

\subsection{Summary of Key Findings}
Our primary finding corroborates the central thesis of the reference paper: a U-Net based convolutional encoder is indeed a highly effective architecture for music genre classification. Our champion model, the \texttt{UNet\_Audio\_Classifier}, achieved a mean validation accuracy of \textbf{90.44\%} under a robust 5-fold cross-validation scheme on the GTZAN dataset \textbf{\cite{tzanetakis2002gtzan}}. This result is competitive with state-of-the-art benchmarks and confirms the architecture's capacity to learn discriminative, hierarchical features from audio spectrograms.

However, our second key finding is a significant and irreconcilable discrepancy between our results and the 99.41\% accuracy reported by Patil et al. This leads us to hypothesize that the performance gap is not attributable to a flaw in our replication, but rather to potential methodological issues in the original study, most likely related to data partitioning and information leakage \textbf{\cite{salamon2017data_leakage}}. %<-- Citazione finale che rafforza l'ipotesi principale.
Consequently, a principal contribution of our work is the establishment of a transparent and—most importantly—reproducible performance benchmark for this architecture.

Furthermore, our extension experiments demonstrated the architecture's adaptability and limitations. The exceptional performance on the Tabla Taala dataset (96.5\% accuracy), achieved via transfer learning \textbf{\cite{choi2017transfer}}, %<-- Citazione per il transfer learning.
provides strong evidence that features learned from broad music corpora can be effectively fine-tuned for highly specialized, domain-specific tasks like rhythmic pattern recognition. Conversely, the modest performance on the complex FMA Small dataset (41.1\% accuracy) \textbf{\cite{defferrard2017fma}} %<-- Citazione FMA.
underscores the challenge that high intra-genre variance poses even to robust models, confirming its status as a more difficult, real-world benchmark.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
A primary limitation of this study is the inherent ambiguity surrounding the "novel mathematical model" described by Patil et al. Without precise, reproducible details of their implementation, our work serves as a replication of the architectural concept rather than its specific, proprietary instantiation.

Based on our findings, several avenues for future work emerge:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Architectural Enhancements for Complex Datasets:} Future work could focus on enhancing model capacity and incorporating more advanced mechanisms, such as attention layers, to address the challenges posed by high-variance datasets like FMA.
    \item \textbf{Advanced Fine-Tuning Strategies:} The success of our simple fine-tuning approach suggests that more sophisticated techniques, such as layer-wise learning rate decay or gradual unfreezing of the encoder backbone, could yield further performance gains on the Indian and Tabla datasets.
    \item \textbf{Broader Domain Adaptation:} Applying this validated U-Net feature extractor to other audio classification tasks, such as environmental sound classification or instrument recognition, would be a valuable extension to further probe the limits of its generalization.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Final Remarks}
% ------------------ GTZAN caveats ------------------
\section*{Limitations of the GTZAN dataset}
The GTZAN dataset is widely used but also well-documented to contain problems that can bias experimental results. Known issues include duplicated excerpts, mislabeled files, and the presence of multiple excerpts from the same recording or artist \cite{sturm2013gtzan}. These problems can inflate reported performance if splits are not performed carefully (e.g., at track or artist level). In this study we explicitly performed track-level splits to mitigate these issues; however, the reader should remain aware that GTZAN's intrinsic limitations make it an imperfect proxy for real-world generalization.
